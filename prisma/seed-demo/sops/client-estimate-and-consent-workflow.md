**Owner:** Client Services · **Review cycle:** Every 12 months · **Applies to:** Client services representatives and any staff who take client authorisation

## Purpose

Turn a clinician's plan into a document the client has actually agreed to. Consent is a conversation with a signature on the end of it, not a form handed across a desk — and this procedure exists because the difference shows up in the discharge conversation, one way or the other.

## Building the estimate

Every estimate has three numbers: the low end, the likely total, and the ceiling the client is authorising. The low end is the figure a client remembers, so it is presented as the *minimum to reach a decision*, never as a total.

| Field | Filled with | Read aloud to the client |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low end | Work that will certainly be done | Yes — as the minimum, in those words |
| Likely total | The plan's realistic cost | Yes |
| Ceiling | The most that may be spent without a new call | Yes — this is what the signature authorises |
| Contingency | Anything the clinician has flagged as possible | Only if it changes the ceiling |

> [!note] The ceiling is the promise the hospital is held to. If the plan grows past it, work stops and the client is called before anything further is done — an overrun the client never approved is a complaint waiting to happen.

:::steps
1. **Receive the plan from the clinician** — by the method the service uses, and never paraphrased from memory.
2. **Convert it into money** — using the current fee schedule, and flag anything the schedule cannot price to the clinician before quoting.
3. **Walk the client through all three numbers** — pause after the ceiling and ask whether it is workable.
4. **Capture consent** — the client signs or accepts electronically, and the record names the person who took it.
5. **File it against the patient** — so the clinician opening the record sees the same ceiling the client agreed to.
6. **Set the update commitment** — who calls the client, and when.
:::

> [!tip] Read the numbers back in reverse before they sign: ceiling, likely total, low end. A client who can repeat the ceiling back has understood it; a client who nods has usually only heard the low end.

## Revisiting an estimate

- A revised estimate supersedes the previous one and is captured the same way, with the same three numbers.
- Declining a recommended next step is a legitimate outcome: the alternatives are offered in writing and the client's choice is recorded.
- A client who is unhappy with the numbers is offered a clinician conversation, not a smaller number invented at the desk.
