**Owner:** Diagnostic Imaging · **Review cycle:** Every 12 months · **Applies to:** Staff who position patients or take radiographs

## Purpose

A radiograph is a small, cumulative radiation dose to staff and a positioning decision for the patient. Both are managed deliberately: nobody is in the beam who does not have to be, and no patient is repositioned by hand when a positioning aid would do the job.

## Dose is a distance problem

Radiation intensity falls off sharply with distance from the source, so the cheapest protection in the room is stepping away. Scatter falls off by roughly the inverse of the square of the distance travelled, which is why the same exposure is dramatically safer at the end of the tether than at the patient's side.[^1]

> [!warning] Personal protective equipment is worn by everyone in the room for every exposure, including the person holding the plate. Lead gloves and a thyroid shield have no substitute, and "just this one film" is not a policy.

## Every exposure

:::steps
1. **Gown for the room** — lead apron, thyroid shield, and gloves for anyone remaining inside the door.
2. **Use a positioning aid wherever possible** — sandbags, troughs, and ties before hands. Hands are the last resort.
3. **Announce the exposure** — "film up" in a clear voice, confirm the room is clear, then fire.
4. **Step to the tether or behind the barrier** — the minimum safe distance is the one at the end of the cord, not arm's length.
5. **Review the image before releasing the patient** — a poor film repeated twice is more dose than a correctly positioned one taken once.
:::

## When a manual hold is unavoidable

A manual holder is a second staff member whose only job is the patient, never the machine. The holder states out loud that they are holding, and it is recorded on the exposure log. No member of staff holds a patient for radiographs repeatedly within a shift — the rotation is the radiology lead's responsibility, not the individual's.

> [!critical] Never hold a patient without a lead apron and gloves because the patient "is only small" or the film "is only a quick one". The dose is not proportional to the patient's size, and the film being quick is precisely why the preparation gets skipped.

## Exposure log

Every exposure records the patient, the view, the staff present, and whether a manual holder was used. The log is reviewed monthly by the radiology lead for repeat exposures and manual holds, which are the two signals that positioning training needs attention.

[^1]: Inverse-square behaviour is a property of any point source; the practical rule is simply *double the distance, a quarter the dose*.
